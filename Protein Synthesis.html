Protein Synthesis: The Master Plan

Protein synthesis is the process whereby biological cells generate new proteins. It occurs in two main stages: Transcription and Translation.

Key Formula Flow:
DNA (Gene) → Transcription → mRNA → Translation → Amino Acid Chain (Polypeptide) → Protein

1. Transcription (in the Nucleus)

  • A portion of the DNA double helix unwinds and unzips (hydrogen bonds break) controlled by RNA polymerase.
  • One strand of DNA acts as a template.
  • Free RNA nucleotides in the nucleoplasm build a complementary mRNA strand.
  • Base pairing: Cytosine (C) pairs with Guanine (G), and Adenine (A) pairs with Uracil (U) (Note: RNA has Uracil instead of Thymine).
  • The completed single-stranded mRNA detaches, moves out of the nucleus via nuclear pores, and travels to the ribosome.

2. Translation (at the Ribosome)

  • The mRNA attaches to a ribosome in the cytoplasm.
  • Each sequence of 3 bases on the mRNA is called a codon.
  • tRNA molecules in the cytoplasm carry specific amino acids.
  • Each tRNA has 3 exposed bases called an anticodon which is complementary to the mRNA codon.
  • tRNA brings the required amino acid to the ribosome. Amino acids are linked together by peptide bonds to form a polypeptide chain.

Definitions

  • Codon: Three consecutive nitrogenous bases on mRNA specifying an amino acid.
  • Anticodon: Three consecutive nitrogenous bases on tRNA complementary to a codon.
  • Polypeptide: A chain of many amino acids linked by peptide bonds.

Connections to Other Sections

Understanding protein synthesis requires integrating knowledge from other parts of the syllabus.

Grade 10: Cell Structure

Connection: The Nucleus, Ribosomes, and Cytoplasm. You must know that DNA cannot leave the nucleus because it is too large, hence the need for messenger RNA (mRNA) to carry the code to the ribosomes.

Grade 12: Genetics and Inheritance

Connection: Genes are sections of DNA that code for a particular protein. Mutations (point/frameshift) in the DNA sequence alter the mRNA, which can change the amino acid sequence, resulting in a non-functional protein (e.g., Albinism or Haemophilia).

Grade 12: Meiosis

Connection: Crossing over and random arrangement during meiosis create unique DNA combinations. This unique DNA translates into unique proteins, contributing to continuous variation within a species.

Grade 12: DNA Profiling

Connection: Only coding regions (exons) of DNA are used for protein synthesis. The non-coding regions (introns/STRs) are highly variable and are used extensively in DNA profiling to identify individuals.

IEB Exam Specifics (Key Points)

Crucial Terminology for IEB: Do not say "matches" – use the word "complementary". E.g., "The anticodon on the tRNA is complementary to the codon on the mRNA."
  • Locations are non-negotiable: Transcription = Nucleus. Translation = Cytoplasm (at the Ribosome).
  • Thymine vs. Uracil: Remember that RNA does NOT contain Thymine. If an exam asks for an RNA sequence complementary to DNA 'A', the answer is 'U', not 'T'.
  • Amino Acid Tables: You will often be given a table mapping mRNA codons or DNA triplets to amino acids. Read the question carefully to see if the table provides DNA, mRNA, or tRNA codes before you trace the sequence.
  • Bonds: Know that weak hydrogen bonds hold DNA bases together (and break during transcription), while peptide bonds hold amino acids together.
  • Differences Table: Be prepared to draw a table comparing DNA and RNA (e.g., Double vs. Single stranded, Deoxyribose vs. Ribose sugar, Thymine vs. Uracil).
