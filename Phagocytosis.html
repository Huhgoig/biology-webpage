The Process of Phagocytosis

Phagocytosis (from Greek phago = to devour, cyto = cell) is a form of endocytosis where a cell engulfs large particles or whole cells. In the immune system, specialized white blood cells called phagocytes (such as macrophages and neutrophils) use this process to ingest and destroy pathogens like bacteria.

The Phagocytosis Sequence:
Pathogen → Chemotaxis → Adherence → Phagosome → Phagolysosome → Digestion → Exocytosis

Key Definitions

  • Macrophage: A large, non-specific phagocytic white blood cell found in tissues.
  • Pathogen: A disease-causing microorganism (e.g., bacteria, virus).
  • Pseudopodia: Temporary, arm-like extensions of the cell membrane used to surround the pathogen ("false feet").
  • Phagosome: The intracellular vesicle formed when the cell membrane completely surrounds the engulfed particle.
  • Lysosome: A cellular organelle containing strong hydrolytic (digestive) enzymes.

The 5 Steps of Phagocytosis

  1. Chemotaxis: The phagocyte detects chemical signals released by the pathogen or damaged tissue and moves toward the site of infection.
  2. Adherence (Attachment): The phagocyte binds to specific molecules on the surface of the pathogen. (Opsonins, like antibodies, can coat the pathogen to make this binding easier—a process called opsonization).
  3. Ingestion: The phagocyte extends pseudopodia around the pathogen, engulfing it and enclosing it in a membrane-bound vacuole called a phagosome.
  4. Digestion: Inside the cell, lysosomes fuse with the phagosome to form a phagolysosome. Hydrolytic enzymes (like lysozymes) are released and break down the pathogen.
  5. Elimination (Exocytosis): The indigestible debris and waste products are expelled from the cell via exocytosis.

Connecting the Dots (IEB Focus)

The IEB syllabus requires you to understand how different biological systems and processes interlink. Phagocytosis is an excellent topic for cross-linking concepts.

1. Cell Structure & The Cell Membrane (Grade 10/11)

The Fluid Mosaic Model of the cell membrane is essential here. The membrane must be flexible and fluid to extend pseudopodia and invaginate to form a phagosome. If the membrane were rigid, endocytosis would be impossible.

2. Organelles (Grade 10)

  • Lysosomes: The "stomachs" of the cell. They manufacture and contain hydrolytic enzymes.
  • Mitochondria: Phagocytosis is an active process (active transport/endocytosis). The cytoskeleton requires large amounts of ATP produced by cellular respiration in the mitochondria to move the pseudopodia.
  • Cytoskeleton: Microfilaments actin and myosin contract to change the cell's shape.

3. Enzymes & pH (Grade 11)

The enzymes inside lysosomes (acid hydrolases) work optimally at a low, acidic pH (around pH 4.5 - 5.0). The phagolysosome actively pumps in hydrogen ions (H+) to lower the pH, activating the enzymes to destroy the pathogen. If these enzymes leak into the neutral cytoplasm (pH 7.2), they denature or become inactive, protecting the cell from self-digestion.

4. Transport across Membranes (Grade 10/11)

Phagocytosis is a specific type of Endocytosis (bulk transport into the cell), and the elimination of the waste is Exocytosis (bulk transport out). Both are forms of Active Transport requiring ATP.

Exam Survival Guide: Key Points

  • Phagocytosis is part of the Innate (Non-Specific) Immune System. It responds the same way to all pathogens and has no "memory".
  • Always use correct terminology in exams: use Phagosome (the vesicle with the pathogen) and Phagolysosome (the vesicle after the lysosome fuses).
  • Understand the difference between Endocytosis (entering) and Exocytosis (exiting).
  • Be prepared to interpret diagrams. If you see a cell wrapping "arms" around a bacteria, it's illustrating the Ingestion phase via pseudopodia.
  • In data response questions: A high white blood cell count (specifically neutrophils or macrophages) in a blood test indicates an active infection in the body.

Common Exam Pitfall:

Do not confuse phagocytes with lymphocytes (B-cells and T-cells). Phagocytes engulf and destroy directly (non-specific). Lymphocytes produce antibodies or target specific infected cells (specific/adaptive immunity).
