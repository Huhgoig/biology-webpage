Human Evolution

Grade 12 IEB Life Sciences | Fossil Themed Interactive Lesson

1. Introduction to Human Evolution

Human evolution is the lengthy process of change by which people originated from apelike ancestors. Scientific evidence shows that the physical and behavioral traits shared by all people originated from apelike ancestors and evolved over a period of approximately six million years.

2. Hominid vs Hominin

  • Hominid: The group consisting of all modern and extinct Great Apes (i.e., modern humans, chimpanzees, gorillas, and orangutans plus all their immediate ancestors).
  • Hominin: The group consisting of modern humans, extinct human species, and all our immediate bipedal ancestors (including members of the genera Ardipithecus, Australopithecus, and Homo).

3. Anatomical Differences: Apes vs Hominins

To understand hominin evolution, we look at the structural changes that facilitated bipedalism and dietary shifts.

Bipedalism (Walking on two legs)

  • Foramen Magnum: In apes, it is positioned towards the back of the skull (quadrupedal). In hominins, it is central/forward under the skull to balance the head on the spine.
  • Spine Shape: Apes have a C-shaped spine. Hominins have an S-shaped spine to act as a shock absorber and keep body weight above the pelvis.
  • Pelvis: Apes have a long, narrow pelvis. Hominins have a short, broad, bowl-shaped pelvis to support the upper body weight and for the attachment of large gluteal muscles used in walking.
  • Femur (Thigh bone): In hominins, the femur angles inward (valgus angle) from the hip to the knee, bringing the knees under the body's center of gravity.
  • Feet: Hominins lack an opposable big toe. The toes are aligned, and the foot has a distinctive arch to absorb shock.

Cranium and Diet

  • Cranial Capacity: Apes have smaller brains (approx. 400-500ml). Hominins show an evolutionary trend of increasing brain size (modern humans ~1350ml).
  • Prognathism vs Orthognathism: Apes have protruding jaws (prognathic). Humans have flat faces (orthognathic).
  • Dentition: Apes have large canines, a U-shaped dental arcade, and prominent diastema (gaps for canines). Humans have smaller teeth, smaller canines, a parabolic (V-shaped/rounded) dental arcade, and no diastema.
  • Cranial Ridges: Apes have prominent brow ridges and sagittal crests for attachment of strong chewing muscles. Humans lack these due to a softer diet and cooked food.

4. Key Hominin Genera

Ardipithecus (e.g., A. ramidus)

Lived ~4.4 million years ago in Africa. Shows evidence of early bipedalism (pelvis) but still had an opposable big toe and long arms for climbing trees.

Australopithecus

Lived ~4 to 2 million years ago in Africa. Fully bipedal but had ape-like skulls and small brains.

  • A. afarensis (Lucy): Found in East Africa. Clear evidence of bipedalism (Laetoli footprints).
  • A. africanus: Found in South Africa (Taung Child, Mrs. Ples).
  • A. sediba: Found in Malapa, SA (Karabo). Shows transitional features between Australopithecus and Homo.

Homo

Characterized by larger brains, tool making, and eventually migrating out of Africa.

  • Homo habilis: "Handy man". First consistent tool users (Oldowan tools).
  • Homo erectus: First to leave Africa. Used fire, more complex tools (Acheulian). Larger brain.
  • Homo neanderthalensis: Lived in Europe/Asia. Adapted to cold. Buried their dead (cultural rituals).
  • Homo sapiens: Modern humans. Originated in Africa ~300,000 years ago. High forehead, prominent chin.

5. The Out of Africa Hypothesis

States that modern humans (Homo sapiens) evolved in Africa roughly 200,000 - 300,000 years ago, and then migrated outwards to populate the rest of the world, replacing other early hominin species like Neanderthals and H. erectus.

Evidence:

  • Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA): Passed only from the mother. Shows the highest genetic diversity is in Africa, indicating the oldest lineage (Mitochondrial Eve).
  • Y-Chromosome DNA: Passed from father to son. Traces back to a "Y-chromosome Adam" in Africa.
  • Fossil Evidence: The oldest H. sapiens fossils are found in Africa (e.g., Omo Kibish, Florisbad, Klasies River).

Connections to Other Biology Sections

Integrating human evolution with other sections of the IEB syllabus is crucial for Section C essay questions.

1. Genetics and DNA Profile

Connection: How do we trace ancestry?

Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) mutates at a known, steady rate (molecular clock). Because it doesn't undergo crossing over during meiosis (only maternal inheritance), mutations are the only source of variation. By comparing the number of mutations between different populations, geneticists can calculate how long ago they shared a common ancestor.

2. Meiosis

Connection: Sources of variation.

Evolution relies on variation within a population. Meiosis provides this through Crossing Over (Prophase I) and Random Arrangement of chromosomes (Metaphase I). Without meiosis generating unique gametes, natural selection would have no variation to act upon.

3. Natural Selection

Connection: The mechanism of change.

Bipedalism evolved via Natural Selection. As the African climate cooled and dried, dense forests turned into open savannas. Hominins with mutations allowing better upright walking could see over tall grass (to spot predators/prey), free their hands to carry food/tools, and reduce the body surface area exposed to the hot sun. They survived, reproduced, and passed on the alleles for bipedalism.

4. Speciation

Connection: How did new hominin species arise?

Allopatric speciation played a role. Populations of early hominins became separated by geographical barriers (like the Great Rift Valley). Different environmental pressures on either side led to natural selection acting differently on the populations. Over time, they became genotypically and phenotypically distinct, resulting in new species (e.g., various Australopithecus species).

Key Points for IEB Exams

  • Always know the differences between apes and hominins: Focus on the Foramen Magnum, Pelvis shape, Spine shape, and Dentition. (Often a table question).
  • Cultural vs Biological Evolution: Biological is physical changes (brain size, pelvis). Cultural is passing down knowledge (tool making, fire, art). Homo habilis is the start of significant cultural evolution (Oldowan tools).
  • South African Fossil Sites: You must know the Cradle of Humankind (Sterkfontein), and key fossils like Taung Child, Mrs Ples, Little Foot, and Karabo.
  • Out of Africa Hypothesis vs Multiregional Hypothesis: Out of Africa is supported by mtDNA and fossil evidence. Multiregional (less supported now) suggests H. erectus left Africa and evolved into H. sapiens simultaneously in different parts of the world.
  • Diagram interpretation: Be prepared to identify the evolutionary trend from a series of skulls (look for decreasing brow ridge, increasing brain case, flatter face, smaller canines).
