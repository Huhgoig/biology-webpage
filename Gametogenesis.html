Gametogenesis Overview

Gametogenesis is the biological process by which diploid or haploid precursor cells undergo cell division and differentiation to form mature haploid gametes. In humans, this occurs via meiosis.

Spermatogenesis (Males)

Occurs in the seminiferous tubules of the testes. It begins at puberty and continues throughout life.

  1. Multiplication phase: Cells of the germinal epithelium divide by mitosis to form diploid spermatogonia.
  2. Growth phase: Spermatogonia grow and become primary spermatocytes (2n).
  3. Maturation phase (Meiosis):
    • Meiosis I: Primary spermatocytes divide to form two haploid secondary spermatocytes (n).
    • Meiosis II: Secondary spermatocytes divide to form four haploid spermatids (n).
  4. Differentiation: Spermatids undergo physical changes (develop a tail, acrosome, and midpiece packed with mitochondria) to become mature spermatozoa (sperm cells).

Oogenesis (Females)

Occurs in the ovaries. It begins before birth, pauses, resumes at puberty, and ends at menopause.

  1. Before Birth: Germinal epithelium cells undergo mitosis to form diploid oogonia. These grow into primary oocytes (2n) and begin Meiosis I, but pause in Prophase I. They are enclosed in primary follicles.
  2. Puberty onwards: Each month under FSH influence, a primary oocyte completes Meiosis I to form a large secondary oocyte (n) and a small first polar body (n).
  3. Ovulation: The secondary oocyte is released. It begins Meiosis II but pauses at Metaphase II.
  4. Fertilization: Only if a sperm enters the secondary oocyte does it complete Meiosis II, forming a mature ovum (n) and a second polar body.

Key Differences

Feature Spermatogenesis Oogenesis
Number of gametes 4 viable sperm per primary cell 1 viable ovum (and 2-3 polar bodies)
Timing Continuous from puberty Cyclical, begins before birth, pauses
Size of gametes Small, motile Large, stationary (contains yolk/cytoplasm)

Biological Connections

Understanding how Gametogenesis links to other sections helps with higher-order exam questions.

1. Meiosis

Gametogenesis relies completely on Meiosis. The reduction division ensures that gametes are haploid, preventing the doubling of chromosomes at fertilization. Crossing over (Prophase I) and random arrangement (Metaphase I & II) in the testes/ovaries ensure genetic variation in the resulting sperm and ova.

2. The Endocrine System (Hormones)

The process is hormonally driven:

  • FSH (Follicle Stimulating Hormone): Stimulates spermatogenesis in males and the development of the primary follicle (containing the oocyte) in females.
  • LH (Luteinizing Hormone): Stimulates testosterone production in males, and causes ovulation of the secondary oocyte in females.
  • Testosterone / Oestrogen: Essential for the maturation of the gametes and the reproductive systems.

3. Genetics and Inheritance

The alleles separated during Meiosis (Mendel's Law of Segregation) end up in different gametes. The fusing of a specific sperm with a specific ovum determines the unique genotype of the offspring, including sex determination (X or Y carrying sperm).

Key Exam Points (IEB Focus)

  • Ploidy is crucial: Always specify if a cell is diploid (2n) or haploid (n). Germinal epithelium, spermatogonia, and primary spermatocytes/oocytes are diploid. Secondary spermatocytes/oocytes, spermatids, sperm, and ova are haploid.
  • Polar bodies: Know why they form. Unequal cytokinesis during oogenesis ensures the ovum retains almost all the cytoplasm and nutrients needed for initial embryonic development, while discarding extra chromosomes into polar bodies.
  • Location: Be precise. Spermatogenesis is in the seminiferous tubules of the testes. Oogenesis is in the follicles of the ovaries.
  • Timeline differences: A common long question asks you to contrast the continuous, post-puberty nature of spermatogenesis with the paused, cyclical nature of oogenesis.
  • Structure of Sperm: Acrosome (enzymes to penetrate egg), Nucleus (haploid DNA), Midpiece (mitochondria for ATP/energy), Tail (flagellum for swimming).
