Life on Land: Ecological Succession

Grade 11 IEB Life Sciences Interactive Learning Module

1. Introduction to Ecological Succession

Ecological Succession is the predictable, gradual, and sequential process by which ecosystem structures change and develop over time following a disturbance or creation of new substrate. It represents the dynamics of species replacement within a community on land (terrestrial biomes).

Key Definition: Primary Succession
Succession that occurs on previously completely uninhabited, barren land surfaces lacking true soil (e.g., bare rock, cooled volcanic lava, sand dunes, exposed glacial till).
Key Definition: Secondary Succession
Succession that occurs in an area where an existing biological community has been cleared or disturbed, but where fertile soil and seeds/spores remain intact (e.g., abandoned farmland, deforested areas, post-wildfire zones).

2. Stages of Primary Succession

  1. Nudation & Arrival: Starts with an inorganic, sterile environment. Pioneer species arrive via wind, water, or animal vectors.
  2. Pioneer Stage: Pioneer species (e.g., crustose lichens) secrete organic acids that chemically weather rock. As pioneers die and decompose, they contribute organic matter (humus), forming a primitive layer of soil.
  3. Establishment of Mosses and Herbaceous Plants: Shallow soil supports mosses, which trap more moisture and dust. Roots cause physical weathering. Soil depth increases.
  4. Seral Stages (Intermediate Species): As soil thickens and nutrient availability rises, wind-blown seeds of grasses, herbs, and eventually woody shrubs germinate. Microclimate becomes shaded, cooler, and more humid. Pioneer species are outcompeted due to shade.
  5. Climax Community: Shade-tolerant trees mature to create a stable, self-perpetuating climax community. Biomass, biodiversity, and ecosystem complexity reach dynamic equilibrium.

3. Stages of Secondary Succession

Secondary succession skips the lengthy soil-formation phase because soil, seed banks, and underground root systems are already present. Consequently, secondary succession progresses much faster (decades instead of centuries).

  • Disturbance Event: Fire, deforestation, agricultural clearance, or flooding wipes out existing vegetation.
  • Pioneer Stage (Secondary): Fast-growing, r-selected weed species and opportunistic annual grasses rapidly colonize the nutrient-rich, exposed soil.
  • Intermediate Stage: Perennial grasses, shrubs, and pioneer trees (e.g., pine trees) replace annual plants.
  • Climax Community: Hardwood trees establish, restoring the climax community state specific to that biome.

4. Key Equations & Quantitative Concepts in Succession

IEB Life Sciences emphasizes understanding energy flow, biomass, and population changes quantitatively during succession.

Net Primary Productivity (NPP) & Gross Primary Productivity (GPP):
NPP = GPP - R
Where:
- GPP = Total solar energy converted into chemical energy via photosynthesis.
- R = Respiratory losses by autotrophs.
- NPP = Rate at which biomass accumulates in ecosystems (available to consumers).

Trend during succession: In early pioneer stages, GPP > R, meaning NPP is high, leading to rapid accumulation of biomass. In mature climax communities, GPP ~ R, meaning NPP approaches zero as total biomass reaches a steady state.

Species Diversity Indices (e.g., Simpson's Diversity Index [SD]):
SD = 1 - sum[n(n-1)]/N(N-1)
Where:
- n = total number of organisms of a particular species.
- N = total number of organisms of all species.

Trend: Species richness (N) and diversity index (SD) increase during early and middle seral stages, stabilizing or slightly decreasing at climax as dominant trees shade out understory species.

5. Comparison: Primary vs. Secondary Succession

Feature Primary Succession Secondary Succession
Starting Point Bare rock / sterile environment (No soil) Pre-existing soil intact with seed banks
Pioneer Species Lichens, mosses, cyanobacteria Annual weeds, grasses, sprouted roots
Time Taken Very slow (Hundreds to thousands of years) Fast (50 to 200 years)
Humus / Soil Depth Initially absent; builds up gradually Already thick and nutrient-rich
Trigger / Origin Volcanic eruptions, glacier retreats, new sand dunes Wildfires, forest clearing, abandoned farms

High-Yield Exam Focus (IEB Grade 11)

Examiners frequently assess specific conceptual nuances, trends, and analytical questions on plant succession. Focus on these core aspects:

1. Changes in Ecosystem Characteristics During Succession
  • Biomass: Increases from zero to high, plateauing at climax.
  • Biodiversity / Species Richness: Increases initially, reaches maximum in mid-to-late seral stages, and slightly stabilizes at climax due to dominance by canopy trees.
  • Soil Depth & Organic Matter: Steadily increases; nutrient retention capacity improves.
  • Food Web Complexity: Transitions from simple linear food chains to complex, interconnected food webs with higher trophic stability.
  • Abiotic Microclimate: Moisture increases, soil erosion decreases, wind speed at ground level drops, and light intensity at ground level decreases due to canopy shading.
2. Biological Strategies: r-selected vs. K-selected Species
  • Pioneers are typical r-strategists: Small size, rapid growth, short lifespans, produce massive numbers of wind-dispersed seeds/spores, low competitive ability.
  • Climax species are typical K-strategists: Large body/plant size, slow growth rates, long lifespans, produce fewer large seeds, highly competitive in shade and stable environments.
3. Common IEB Exam Traps & Misconceptions
  • Misconception: Climax communities never change.
    Fact: Climax communities exist in dynamic equilibrium—they fluctuate around a stable mean and can revert to earlier seral stages if localized disturbances (e.g., tree falls, fires) occur.
  • Misconception: Lichens are just plants.
    Fact: Lichens are mutualistic symbiotic organisms composed of a fungus and an alga or cyanobacterium.
  • Misconception: Secondary succession starts from zero.
    Fact: Secondary succession starts with soil, organic matter, microbial life, and seed banks already present.

Synoptic Biology Connections (IEB Curriculum Integration)

To score top marks in IEB Life Sciences, you must link succession concepts to broader topics within the Grade 11 syllabus.

1. Link to Population Ecology (r- vs K-Selection)

Succession represents a gradual transition from ecosystems dominated by r-selected opportunists to those dominated by K-selected competitors. Early pioneers focus energy on rapid reproduction to exploit unoccupied niches, whereas climax trees allocate energy toward structural maintenance (lignin, trunks), root systems, and shade tolerance to outcompete neighbors near environmental carrying capacity ($K$).

2. Link to Soil Science & Biogeochemical Cycles

  • Nitrogen Cycle: Primary pioneer lichens and actinobacteria often fix atmospheric nitrogen ($N_2 \rightarrow NH_4^+$), enriching sterile rock so nitrifying bacteria can convert it into nitrates ($NO_3^-$) usable by incoming plants.
  • Carbon Cycle: As succession advances, the ecosystem shifts from a net carbon source/neutral area to a major carbon sink through extensive photosynthetic carbon fixation into wood/lignin biomass.

3. Link to Biomes & Life on Land

The specific climax community achieved in any region is ultimately constrained by macroclimate factors—specifically **temperature** and **annual rainfall**. For example, primary succession on bare granite in the Fynbos biome will lead to a fynbos shrub climax, whereas in KwaZulu-Natal, it will lead to a Subtropical Forest climax.

4. Link to Plant Anatomy & Diversity

Morphological adaptations evolve along the successional gradient:

  • Pioneers (Bryophytes/Lichens): Lacks true vascular tissue, possess rhizoids, poikilohydric (desiccation tolerant).
  • Intermediate (Pteridophytes/Gymnosperms): Development of vascular tissue (xylem/phloem), true roots, cuticle protection.
  • Climax (Angiosperm Trees): Deep tap root systems, extensive secondary growth (xylem ring formation), seed protection within fruits.
