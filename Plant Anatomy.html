1. Plant Tissues

Plant tissues are broadly divided into Meristematic (actively dividing) and Permanent (differentiated) tissues.

Permanent Tissues Overview

Tissue Structure Function
Epidermis Single layer of cells on the outside. Often covered by a waxy cuticle. Root epidermis has root hairs. Protection, prevention of water loss. Absorption in roots.
Parenchyma Thin-walled, large vacuoles, intercellular spaces. Packing tissue, storage of starch/water. Chlorenchyma (contains chloroplasts) does photosynthesis.
Collenchyma Unevenly thickened cell walls (thick at corners). Provides flexible mechanical support in young growing stems and leaves.
Sclerenchyma Evenly thickened, lignified cell walls. Dead at maturity. Fibres and sclereids. Provides rigid mechanical support and protection.

Vascular Tissues (Transport)

Xylem (Red/Pink in diagrams): Transports water and dissolved minerals upwards from roots to leaves. Composed of vessels (continuous tubes) and tracheids. Dead, hollow, and lignified (provides support).

Phloem (Blue in diagrams): Transports manufactured organic food (sucrose) from leaves to the rest of the plant. Composed of sieve tubes (no nucleus) and companion cells (control sieve tubes). Living tissue.

2. Anatomy of Dicotyledonous Organs

The Root

The root absorbs water/minerals and anchors the plant. Notice the central "star" shape which is characteristic of a dicot root.


Figure 1: Transverse Section (T.S.) of a Dicotyledonous Root
  • Casparian Strip: A waterproof band of suberin in the endodermis that forces water to pass through the cell membranes, allowing the plant to control mineral uptake.

The Stem

The dicot stem has vascular bundles arranged in a ring (unlike monocots, which are scattered). This ring separates the outer cortex from the inner pith.


Figure 2: Transverse Section (T.S.) of a Dicotyledonous Stem
  • Secondary Growth: The fascicular cambium (between xylem and phloem in the bundle) and interfascicular cambium (between bundles) join to form a continuous cambium ring. It produces secondary xylem inwards (wood) and secondary phloem outwards (bark).

The Leaf

Designed for photosynthesis and gas exchange.


Figure 3: Transverse Section (T.S.) of a Dicotyledonous Leaf
  • Upper Epidermis: Transparent, covered in waxy cuticle to prevent water loss while letting light through.
  • Palisade Mesophyll: Tightly packed, elongated cells rich in chloroplasts. Main site of photosynthesis.
  • Spongy Mesophyll: Loosely packed cells with large air spaces for rapid diffusion of CO2,, O2, and water vapour.
  • Stomata: Pores flanked by guard cells, mostly on the lower epidermis. Regulate gas exchange and transpiration.

3. Water Movement Formulas & Concepts

Transpiration Pull: The main force pulling water up. Evaporation of water from leaves creates a negative pressure (tension) in the xylem.
Cohesion: Water molecules stick to each other (hydrogen bonding).
Adhesion: Water molecules stick to the sides of the xylem vessels.

🧠 Neural Connections: Linking the Syllabus

The IEB syllabus rewards students who can link different chapters together. Here is how Plant Anatomy connects to other sections:

1. Plant Anatomy ↔ Photosynthesis (Grade 11)

The Link: Leaf structure dictates photosynthetic efficiency. The palisade mesophyll is packed at the top to catch maximum light. The xylem brings the water (reactant for the light-dependent phase), and the phloem removes the glucose (converted to sucrose) produced in the light-independent phase.

2. Plant Anatomy ↔ Cellular Respiration (Grade 11)

The Link: Roots are underground and cannot photosynthesize. They require oxygen for aerobic respiration to generate ATP. This ATP is crucial for the active transport of mineral ions into the root hairs against the concentration gradient.

3. Plant Anatomy ↔ Human Transport Systems (Grade 10/11)

The Link: Compare xylem and blood vessels. Xylem relies on a passive "pull" (transpiration) and has dead cells. Human arteries rely on an active "push" (heart pump) and are made of living muscle tissue. Both use tubes to transport fluids in mass flow.

4. Plant Anatomy ↔ Support Systems (Grade 10)

The Link: Contrast plant support with human skeletons. Plants rely on turgor pressure (in parenchyma) and thickened cell walls (lignin in sclerenchyma and xylem). Humans rely on a calcium-phosphate internal bony skeleton.

🎯 Exam Focus: Key Points to Memorise

When preparing for your IEB papers, ensure you have mastered these highly examinable components:

  • Drawings: Be able to draw and label a transverse section (T.S.) of a dicot root, stem, and leaf. (Rules: use a sharp pencil, continuous lines, no shading, label lines must be drawn with a ruler and not cross).
  • Tissue Identification: Be able to identify collenchyma (thick corners) vs. sclerenchyma (thick everywhere, narrow lumen) from micrographs.
  • Xylem Adaptations:
    • Dead and hollow = no resistance to water flow.
    • Lignified walls = prevents collapse under tension (negative pressure).
    • Pits = allows lateral movement of water.
    • End walls broken down = forms continuous tubes.
  • The Casparian Strip: Examiners love asking about its role. Remember: it blocks the apoplastic (cell wall) pathway, forcing water into the symplastic (cytoplasm) pathway, allowing the endodermis to selectively control mineral uptake.
  • Guard Cells: Understand the mechanism of stomatal opening. When guard cells gain water (turgid), their unequal wall thickening causes them to bow outward, opening the pore. When flaccid, the pore closes.
  • Secondary Growth: Understand the sequence: Cambium forms a ring -> produces secondary xylem inwards -> secondary phloem outwards -> annual rings are formed due to seasonal differences in xylem vessel size.
