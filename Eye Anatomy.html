Anatomy of the Human Eye


Figure 1: Anatomy of the Human Eye

The human eye is a specialized sense organ that responds to light and transmits impulses to the brain to produce the sensation of sight. It is composed of three main layers.

1. The Outer Layer (Fibrous Tunic)

  • Sclera: The tough, white, inelastic outer layer that maintains the shape of the eye and protects internal structures.
  • Cornea: The transparent, convex continuation of the sclera at the front. It allows light to enter and causes initial refraction (bending) of light.

2. The Middle Layer (Vascular Tunic)

  • Choroid: A dark, pigmented, highly vascular layer. The melanin absorbs stray light to prevent reflection within the eye, and blood vessels supply nutrients and oxygen to the retina.
  • Ciliary Body: A thickened extension of the choroid containing ciliary muscles, which control the curvature of the lens during accommodation.
  • Iris: The colored part of the eye, containing circular and radial muscles that control the size of the pupil (Pupillary Mechanism).
  • Pupil: The opening in the center of the iris that lets light in.

3. The Inner Layer (Nervous Tunic)

  • Retina: The light-sensitive layer containing photoreceptors.
    • Rods: Function in dim light, provide black-and-white vision, and peripheral vision.
    • Cones: Function in bright light, provide color vision, and sharp central vision.
  • Macula Lutea (Yellow Spot): The area of the retina with the highest concentration of cones; responsible for the sharpest vision. The central depression is the fovea centralis.
  • Blind Spot: The point where the optic nerve leaves the eye. It has no rods or cones, so no image is formed here.

Other Vital Structures

  • Lens: A biconvex, transparent, flexible structure suspended by suspensory ligaments. It fine-tunes the focusing of light onto the retina.
  • Aqueous Humor: A watery fluid in the anterior chamber maintaining corneal shape.
  • Vitreous Humor: A jelly-like substance in the posterior chamber keeping the eyeball spherical and holding the retina flat against the choroid.
  • Optic Nerve: Transmits nerve impulses from the retina to the brain.

Key Process: Accommodation

The adjustment of the shape of the lens to focus on objects at different distances.

  • Distant Vision ( > 6m): Ciliary muscles relax → suspensory ligaments become taut (tight) → tension is exerted on the lens → lens becomes less convex (flatter) → refractive power decreases.
  • Near Vision ( < 6m): Ciliary muscles contract → tension on suspensory ligaments decreases (they slacken) → lens bulges (becomes more convex due to its own elasticity) → refractive power increases.

Key Process: Pupillary Mechanism

A reflex action that controls the amount of light entering the eye.

  • Bright Light: Circular muscles of the iris contract, radial muscles relax. Pupil constricts (gets smaller) to prevent damage to the retina.
  • Dim Light: Radial muscles contract, circular muscles relax. Pupil dilates (gets larger) to allow more light in.

Key Exam Focus Areas (IEB Specific)

Distinguishing the Mechanisms

A common exam trap is confusing the structures involved in the two main mechanisms. Remember this rule of thumb:

  • Pupillary Mechanism relies on the Iris (Circular and Radial muscles). It responds to Light Intensity.
  • Accommodation relies on the Ciliary Body (Ciliary muscles) and Suspensory Ligaments. It responds to Distance.

Visual Defects

You must be able to identify these, explain their anatomical causes, and state the corrective lens.

Defect Cause / Effect Correction
Short-sightedness (Myopia) Eyeball is too long or cornea too curved. Image falls in front of the retina. Cannot see distant objects clearly. Concave lens (diverges light before it enters the eye).
Long-sightedness (Hypermetropia) Eyeball is too short or cornea too flat. Image falls behind the retina. Cannot see near objects clearly. Convex lens (converges light before it enters the eye).
Astigmatism Irregularly shaped cornea or lens. Causes distorted/blurred vision at all distances. Cylindrical lenses or surgery.
Cataracts Lens becomes opaque/cloudy due to protein clumping (aging, UV exposure). Surgery to replace the lens with an artificial intraocular lens.

Connecting Eye Anatomy to Other Biological Systems

In the IEB exams, questions often link the eye to other chapters. Use these connections to deepen your understanding.

1. The Nervous System: Reflex Arcs

The pupillary mechanism is a classic autonomic reflex arc:

  • Stimulus: Bright light.
  • Receptor: Photoreceptors (rods/cones) in the retina generate an impulse.
  • Sensory Neuron: Carries the impulse via the optic nerve to the brain.
  • Control Center: The midbrain processes the information.
  • Motor Neuron: Carries the impulse to the effector.
  • Effector: Circular muscles of the iris contract (radial muscles relax).
  • Response: Pupil constricts.

2. The Central Nervous System: Brain Lobes

While the eye captures the light, vision happens in the brain. Impulses travel via the optic nerve to the Occipital Lobe of the cerebrum, where they are interpreted (right side up, color, depth perception).

3. The Endocrine System: Adrenaline

During a "fight or flight" response, the adrenal glands secrete adrenaline. Adrenaline stimulates the sympathetic nervous system, causing the radial muscles of the iris to contract. This dilates the pupils, allowing more light in to improve peripheral vision and threat detection, overriding normal light-reflexes temporarily.

4. Homeostasis and Tissues

The cornea lacks blood vessels (avascular) to remain transparent. It gets its nutrients via diffusion from the Aqueous Humor and oxygen directly from the air. Tears (produced by lacrimal glands) keep the outer surface lubricated and contain lysozymes to destroy bacteria.
