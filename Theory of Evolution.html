Evolution Tutor

IEB Grade 12 Life Sciences Interactive Guide

1. Introduction to Evolution

Evolution is the process of change in the inherited characteristics of biological populations over successive generations. It explains the diversity of life on Earth.

2. Evidence for Evolution

  • Fossil Record: Shows gradual changes in organisms over time (e.g., transition fossils like Archaeopteryx).
  • Biogeography: Geographic distribution of species (e.g., ratites like ostriches, emus, rheas separated by continental drift).
  • Genetics (DNA): Similarities in DNA sequences indicate common ancestry.
  • Comparative Anatomy:
    • Homologous structures: Same basic structure, different functions (e.g., human arm, whale flipper). Indicates common ancestor.
    • Analogous structures: Different basic structure, same function (e.g., bird wing, insect wing). Indicates convergent evolution, not recent common ancestry.
    • Vestigial structures: Structures with no apparent function (e.g., human appendix).

3. Lamarck vs Darwin

Jean-Baptiste Lamarck (Incorrect but historically significant)

  • Law of Use and Disuse: If an organism uses a trait, it develops; if not, it reduces.
  • Inheritance of Acquired Characteristics: Traits developed during a lifetime are passed to offspring (e.g., Giraffes stretching necks).
  • Why it's wrong: Phenotypic changes acquired during life are not written into DNA and cannot be inherited.

Charles Darwin (Theory of Evolution by Natural Selection)

  1. Organisms produce more offspring than the environment can support.
  2. There is a great deal of variation among offspring.
  3. Some variations are favorable, helping organisms survive in a specific environment.
  4. Organisms with favorable traits survive, reproduce, and pass these traits to offspring.
  5. Over time, the proportion of individuals with favorable traits increases in the population.

4. Speciation

Speciation is the evolutionary process by which populations evolve to become distinct species.

  • Allopatric Speciation: Speciation due to a geographic barrier (e.g., river, mountain, ocean).
  • Sympatric Speciation: Speciation in the same geographic area without a physical barrier (often due to polyploidy in plants or behavioral changes).

5. Pace of Evolution

  • Phyletic Gradualism: Evolution occurs at a slow, constant, gradual rate. Transitional fossils should be abundant.
  • Punctuated Equilibrium (Eldredge & Gould): Evolution consists of long periods of stasis (no change) interrupted by short, rapid bursts of speciation (usually due to sudden environmental change). Explains "gaps" in the fossil record.

6. Artificial vs Natural Selection

  • Natural Selection: Environment acts as the selective force (e.g., peppered moths).
  • Artificial Selection: Humans act as the selective force, breeding for desired traits (e.g., domestic dogs, crop yield). Leads to inbreeding and loss of genetic diversity.

7. Hominid Evolution

Humans (Homo sapiens) belong to the family Hominidae. Key evolutionary trends include:

  • Bipedalism: Walking on two legs. Evidence: Foramen magnum moved to the center/base of the skull; S-shaped spine; broader, shorter pelvis.
  • Cranial Capacity: Increased brain size (from ~400cm³ in Australopithecus to ~1400cm³ in Homo sapiens).
  • Dentition: Decreased canine size, parabolic (C-shaped) jaw instead of U-shaped jaw, loss of pronounced diastema.
  • Brow Ridges: Became less pronounced.

Out of Africa Hypothesis

States that modern humans (Homo sapiens) evolved in Africa roughly 200,000 years ago and migrated outwards, replacing other hominin species (like Neanderthals) worldwide.

Evidence: Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) mutates at a known rate. African populations have the highest genetic diversity (oldest lineages). Y-chromosome tracing shows similar patterns. Fossil evidence (oldest Homo sapiens fossils found in Africa - e.g., Florisbad, Klasies River).

8. Population Genetics (Hardy-Weinberg Principle)

The HW principle states that allele and genotype frequencies in a population will remain constant from generation to generation in the absence of other evolutionary influences (no mutation, random mating, no gene flow, large population, no natural selection).

Allele frequency formula: p + q = 1
Genotype frequency formula: p² + 2pq + q² = 1

Where:
p = frequency of dominant allele
q = frequency of recessive allele
p² = frequency of homozygous dominant genotype
q² = frequency of homozygous recessive genotype
2pq = frequency of heterozygous genotype

Connections to Other Biology Sections

Evolution is the unifying theory of biology. Here is how it connects to other IEB topics:

1. Genetics and Mutations

  • Source of New Alleles: Mutations (point or frameshift) are the ONLY source of entirely new alleles. Without mutations, there would be no new variation for Natural Selection to act upon.
  • Polygenic Inheritance: Many evolutionary traits (like human height or skin color) show continuous variation, which is controlled by multiple genes (polygenes) interacting with the environment.

2. Meiosis and Reproduction

  • Crossing Over (Prophase I): Exchange of genetic material between homologous chromosomes creates recombinant chromatids, increasing genetic variation in gametes.
  • Random Arrangement (Metaphase I & II): Chromosomes align randomly at the equator, leading to millions of possible combinations in sperm and egg cells.
  • Random Fertilization: Any sperm can fuse with any egg, multiplying the variation. Variation is essential for natural selection.

3. DNA Profiling & Inheritance

  • Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA): Used as evidence for the "Out of Africa" hypothesis. Because mtDNA is inherited strictly through the maternal line without recombination, mutations track maternal lineage directly back to "Mitochondrial Eve" in Africa.

4. Human Impact on the Environment

  • Artificial Selection vs Biodiversity: Crop monocultures and intense selective breeding reduce the genetic gene pool. If an environmental change or disease occurs, the entire population could be wiped out because they lack variation.

High-Yield Exam Points & Pitfalls

The 4-Step "Speciation" Answer Template

If you are asked to describe how a new species forms via geographic isolation (Allopatric Speciation), ALWAYS use this structure for maximum marks:

  1. The population is separated by a geographic barrier (name it if given in the scenario) and splits into two populations.
  2. There is now no gene flow between the two populations.
  3. Since each population experiences different environmental conditions, natural selection occurs independently in each population.
  4. The individuals of the two populations become very different from each other genotypically and phenotypically.
  5. Even if the barrier is removed, they are no longer able to interbreed to produce fertile offspring. We say they are reproductively isolated and a new species has formed.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

  • Lamarckian wording: Never say "the organism mutated in order to survive" or "the giraffe stretched its neck and passed it on." Mutations are random; organisms don't mutate on purpose.
  • Evolution vs Natural Selection: Natural selection is the mechanism; Evolution is the outcome.
  • Antibiotic Resistance: Bacteria do NOT become immune. They become resistant. The antibiotic acts as the selective pressure. The resistant ones survive, reproduce, and pass the resistance gene to the next generation.
  • Foramen Magnum: Don't just say "it moved." Say "moved to a more forward/central position at the base of the skull to support the skull vertically on the spine for bipedalism."
