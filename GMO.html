Genetically Modified Organisms (GMOs)

Definition: A Genetically Modified Organism (GMO) is any organism whose genetic material has been altered using genetic engineering techniques (Recombinant DNA technology).

Artificial Selection vs Genetic Engineering

Artificial Selection Genetic Engineering
Breeding organisms with desired traits over many generations. Directly manipulating an organism's DNA, usually inserting genes from another species.
Slow process (takes years/decades). Fast process (immediate in the resulting organism).
Genes exchanged between closely related species only. Genes can be exchanged between completely unrelated species (e.g., bacteria and plants).

Recombinant DNA Technology: Step-by-Step

The process of introducing a foreign gene into an organism to create a GMO.

  1. Isolation: The target gene (e.g., human insulin gene) is identified and isolated from the donor DNA.
  2. Cutting: Restriction enzymes act as chemical scissors to cut the DNA at specific sequences, creating "sticky ends". The same enzyme is used to cut the plasmid (vector) from a bacterium.
  3. Insertion: The target gene is inserted into the bacterial plasmid.
  4. Ligation: The enzyme DNA Ligase glues the sticky ends together, forming Recombinant DNA.
  5. Transformation: The recombinant plasmid is re-inserted into the host bacterium (e.g., E. coli).
  6. Cloning & Expression: The bacteria multiply rapidly, copying the new gene and producing the desired protein (e.g., insulin).

Examples of GMOs

  • Bt Corn/Cotton: A gene from the bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis is inserted into the plant, causing it to produce a toxin that kills specific pests (borers), reducing the need for chemical pesticides.
  • Golden Rice: Modified with genes to produce beta-carotene (Vitamin A precursor) to prevent nutritional blindness in developing countries.
  • Insulin Production: Human insulin gene inserted into bacteria to mass-produce pure, safe insulin for diabetics.

Pros and Cons of GMOs

Advantages (Pros)

  • Increased crop yields and food security.
  • Reduced use of harmful chemical pesticides (e.g., Bt crops).
  • Enhanced nutritional value (e.g., Golden Rice).
  • Production of medical treatments (insulin, vaccines).
  • Drought/frost resistance.

Disadvantages & Concerns

  • Potential unknown long-term health effects (allergens).
  • Environmental impact: creation of "superweeds" or harm to non-target insects.
  • Loss of biodiversity in agriculture.
  • Economic concerns: farmers must buy expensive seeds annually from biotech monopolies.
  • Ethical/religious objections to "playing God".

Key Components for IEB Exams

Pay special attention to these areas, as they are frequently tested in Paper 2:

1. Process Explanations

Be able to sequentially list and describe the steps of creating a recombinant plasmid. Keywords: Restriction enzymes, DNA ligase, Vector, Plasmid, Sticky ends.

2. Argumentative/Discursive Questions

IEB often asks you to evaluate the ethics or environmental impact of GMOs. You must be able to state both sides (pros and cons) and provide a reasoned conclusion based on biological principles.

3. Terminology

Know the exact definitions of: Transgenic organism, Recombinant DNA, Biotechnology, Artificial selection, Gene therapy.

4. Data Response

You may be given a graph showing the yield of Bt corn vs normal corn or pest populations. Practice analyzing trends, calculating percentage increases, and deducing the effectiveness of the genetic modification.

Connecting GMOs to the Rest of the Syllabus

Understanding how GMOs link to other sections will deepen your insight and help with essay questions.

🧬

DNA, RNA & Protein Synthesis

Genetic engineering relies entirely on the universal nature of the genetic code. A human gene put into a bacterium produces the exact same protein (e.g., insulin) because transcription and translation work the same way in almost all organisms.

🔄

Meiosis & Variation

Natural variation is caused by crossing over and random assortment in meiosis. Genetic engineering is an artificial way of introducing extreme variation (new alleles) that could never occur naturally.

🌍

Evolution & Artificial Selection

Humans have guided evolution for thousands of years via artificial selection (selective breeding). GMOs represent a modern, highly accelerated, and targeted form of human-directed evolution.

📈

Population Ecology

Introducing GMO crops can affect local ecosystems. For instance, herbicide-resistant crops might lead to the natural selection of herbicide-resistant weeds ("superweeds"), demonstrating evolution in action within populations.
